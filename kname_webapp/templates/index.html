First, three things about Korean names
The family name comes first

K-pop’s Jungkook is really 전 Jeon Jung-kook — the family name leads. A Korean name is usually three syllables: a one-syllable family name + a two-syllable given name. And there’s no middle name.

A name is a wish

Most names are written in hanja — Chinese characters chosen for their meaning: light, wisdom, grace. Parents pick them like a blessing. Some names skip hanja and use pure Korean words, like 하늘 Ha-neul (“sky”).

Almost everyone is a Kim, Lee or Park

Just three family names cover nearly half of all Koreans. Three of BTS’s seven members are named Kim — and they aren’t even related. Korea uses only about 300 family names in all.

01 Two Kims usually aren’t related+
A Korean surname isn’t a single family. Each one splits into clans, identified by an ancestral hometown (bon-gwan). The Gimhae Kim and the Gyeongju Kim are completely separate lineages that just happen to share the sound “Kim.” Kim Ji-soo of BLACKPINK and Kim Tae-hyung of BTS are both Kims — and no relation at all. For much of Korean history, two people with the same surname and the same clan seat were considered kin and couldn’t marry. It’s why the surname card you’ll get names a specific clan, not just “Kim.”
02 How names are built from meanings+
In a Sino-Korean name, each of the two given-name syllables is a Chinese character with its own meaning, and parents combine them into a wish — brightness, wisdom, grace, a soaring path. Some families consult a professional naming specialist or check the baby’s birth chart (saju) for balance and good fortune. Others skip Chinese characters entirely and use pure Korean words, like 하늘 Ha-neul (“sky”) or 이슬 I-seul (“dew”). Either way, a Korean name is meant to say something.
03 Why siblings share a syllable+
In many families, everyone of the same generation shares one fixed syllable in their given name — a “generation marker” (dollimja) handed down by the clan, often following a sequence tied to the five elements. Brothers might be Min-jun, Min-seo, Min-jae, or cousins spread across a family all carry the same middle syllable. It lets relatives instantly place which generation someone belongs to. It’s looser in younger families today, but still very much alive.
04 One name, many spellings+
Romanization of Korean names was never standardized, so one surname can be spelled many ways. 이 becomes Lee, Yi, or Rhee; 박 becomes Park, Pak, or Bak; 최 becomes Choi or Choe. Families simply chose a spelling and kept it. That’s also why “Choi” sounds more like “Chweh” than “Choy” — the spelling is a historical accident, not a pronunciation guide.
05 Wives keep their own surname+
A Korean woman keeps her own family name for life — marriage doesn’t change it. The children, though, take their father’s surname. So at home a married couple usually has two different surnames, while the kids share dad’s. For anyone expecting the Western “Mrs. His-Name” pattern, this is often a surprise.
06 Name trends change with the decade+
Popular names cycle by era, just like anywhere. Mid-20th-century women’s names often ended in -ja (Young-ja, Sun-ja), and you’d be hard-pressed to find a baby named that today. Now the favorites are soft, flowing names like Seo-yeon, Ji-woo, and Ha-eun, and hit K-dramas and idols visibly nudge what parents pick. A Korean name quietly carries the decade it was born in.
